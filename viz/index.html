National Survey of Children's Health · 2023–2025

Who's struggling?

A common worry says that mothers working full-time with a baby at home are the ones most at risk. A survey of 88,047 U.S. mothers points somewhere else.

Data story · November 2026 · See the people version

Scroll down ↓

Picture a mother with a baby.

A full-time job. Not enough sleep. Day care pickup at 5:30.

It's easy to assume she's the one most likely to be struggling.

This story checks that picture against the National Survey of Children's Health, which asks parents across the country about their children and themselves. One question asks the parent to rate their own mental or emotional health: excellent, very good, good, fair or poor.

Because the survey is about children, every number here is about children: how many have a mother who answers "fair" or "poor."

Picture 100 children under 6 whose mothers work full-time.

For about 7 of them, mom rates her mental health as fair or poor.

Now sort children by what their mothers do.

Part-time work looks almost the same as full-time: about 8 in 100.

The group that stands out is mothers who are out of work and looking for a job: 15 in 100, about twice the full-time rate.

Mothers who aren't working and aren't looking look like full-time mothers while the kids are little.

Once children reach school age, that starts to change.

By the teenage years, children whose mothers are out of the labor force are as likely to have a mother in fair or poor mental health as children whose mothers are looking for work: about 12 in 100.

Use the age buttons to compare.

Is it really about the job?

These groups of mothers differ in more than work.

Mothers looking for work are about five times as likely as full-time working mothers to live below the poverty line (49% vs. 10%), and less likely to be married (51% vs. 70%). Both go with poorer mental health on their own.

So the next question is what's left when mothers with similar circumstances are compared.

Here is each group's gap compared with full-time mothers, accounting only for the child's age and survey year.

Mothers looking for work: 6.7 points higher.

Now compare mothers with similar incomes, education, marital status, number of kids and age.

The gap for mothers looking for work shrinks to 3.0 points. Smaller, but it doesn't disappear.

For mothers not looking for work, the gap barely moves: 2.5 to 2.7 points.

Add mothers' physical health and both gaps shrink again, to 1.8 and 1.1 points.

But health can be a cause or a result of being out of work, so this view is a check, not the answer.

And the gap between part-time and full-time mothers stays at about zero in every version.

For mothers looking for work, the adjusted gap is about the same at every age: 2 to 4 points.

For mothers not looking for work, it grows with the child.

No gap when the child is under 6. About 3 points at ages 6–11. About 6 points with a teenager.

One reading: staying home with a young child is a common, often chosen arrangement. Being out of the labor force with a teenager more often comes with other circumstances, like health problems or caring for a family member.

The survey can't tell these apart.

Inside the model

Behind those adjusted gaps is one survey-weighted logistic regression. It estimates how each characteristic goes with the odds of fair or poor mental health while holding the others constant.

An odds ratio above 1 means higher odds than the comparison group; below 1, lower odds. A line that crosses 1 means the data can't distinguish the group from the comparison.

Start with work. Holding everything else equal, mothers looking for work have about 1.5 times the odds of fair or poor mental health of full-time mothers; mothers not looking, about 1.4 times.

Part-time work: 1.0, the same as full-time.

Money matters on its own. Every income group below 400% of the poverty line has about 1.6 to 1.7 times the odds of the highest group.

Marital status shows the biggest differences in the model. Never-married and divorced, separated or widowed mothers have more than twice the odds of married mothers.

Education and age matter too: odds are higher without a bachelor's degree and lower for mothers 45 and older.

All of these are associations; none of them shows what causes what.

So how much of the 6.7-point gap between mothers looking for work and full-time mothers do these differences explain?

Build it up one factor at a time, starting from full-time mothers.

Marital status adds the most, then the mother's age and family income. Together the measured differences add up to 2.7 points (41%).

The remaining 4.0 points aren't explained by anything the survey measures.

For mothers not looking for work, the factors pull in opposite directions.

Lower income and less education widen the gap; being more often married narrows it by about as much. Net: almost nothing, which is why adjusting didn't shrink their gap.

Now, the full-time mother with a baby

Back to the picture this story started with.

The survey records each child's exact age, so it can single out mothers whose child is under 2. Among them, 3,753 work full-time.

Among mothers of children under 2, those working full-time are not the ones struggling most.

7.4% rate their mental health fair or poor, inside the range of every other group.

Take a wider slice, anything below "very good," and full-time mothers of babies still sit in the middle.

Compare full-time mothers by their child's age instead.

Those with a child under 2 report slightly better mental health than full-time mothers of older children.

They also report the least parenting aggravation: 1.5%, vs. 4 to 6% for mothers of older children.

(The aggravation questions, such as "this child is much harder to care for than most," may fit older children better than babies.)

So why don't full-time mothers of babies look worse off?

One clue sits in a different question: whether anyone in the family had to quit, turn down or greatly change a job in the past year because of problems with child care.

Where the mother works full-time, about 8 in 100 families had a job change because of child care.

Where she works part-time: about 23.

Where she's looking for work: 30.

Where she's not looking: 15.

This points to sorting. Mothers for whom full-time work and an infant didn't fit may already have cut back or left.

The full-time mothers who remain may be the ones whose child care works.

A one-time survey can't prove it, but sorting like this would hide any strain from full-time work.

And fathers?

The survey also hears from 43,550 fathers, which shows whether these patterns are about mothers or about parents in general.

For mothers, not working goes with a modestly higher rate of fair or poor mental health.

For fathers, the jump is much bigger.

Full-time fathers start lower, at 3 to 4 in 100. Non-employed fathers of school-age kids reach 13 to 14 in 100.

After accounting for income and family circumstances, the fathers' gap (6.7 points) is more than twice the mothers' (2.8).

Studies in Europe link this to the weight of the breadwinner role, though one U.S. study found no difference between men and women.

What this can and can't tell us

Children whose mothers are looking for work are the most likely to have a mother in fair or poor mental health, and family circumstances explain only part of that.

Full-time work, including with a baby at home, does not go with worse mental health in this survey.

But this is one snapshot. It can't say whether work affects mothers' mental health or mental health affects work; it is probably both. The survey has no measure of postpartum depression or anxiety, no work hours and no record of when mothers went back to work.

A better test would use a survey with a depression scale and work hours, such as the National Health Interview Survey.

Methodology

Data. National Survey of Children's Health (NSCH) topical public use files for 2023, 2024 and 2025, pooled (U.S. Census Bureau). One child is sampled per household and an adult who knows the child answers.

Who counts as a mother. The adult who answered is female and the sampled child's biological or adoptive parent (90,415 mothers). Mothers who were "working without pay" (1,361) or had no employment or mental health answer (1,007) were left out, leaving 88,047. Fathers are defined the same way.

Measures. Mental health is the parent's own rating of their mental or emotional health (excellent to poor). Parenting aggravation means "usually" or "always" feeling that the child is much harder to care for than most, does things that really bother the parent, or makes the parent angry. The child care question asks whether anyone in the family had to quit, not take or greatly change a job because of problems with child care in the past 12 months (children 0–5 only).

Estimates. All percentages use the NSCH weights divided by three, with design-based standard errors (strata by year × state × sampling stratum, households as primary sampling units). Because the weights describe children, results read "X% of children have a mother who…". Adjusted results come from survey-weighted logistic regression with family income (six imputations combined with Rubin's rules), education, marital status, number of children, mother's age and survey year. The gap split uses a Fairlie decomposition and is exploratory. Every estimate was checked against the NSCH reliability rules; flagged estimates are marked †.

Unit charts. Dots show weighted percentages rounded to whole numbers out of 100. They are not individual survey respondents.

What this is not. Associations from a cross-sectional survey, not effects of employment.
