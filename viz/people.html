The Room

88,047 mothers and 43,550 fathers, one circle each. One question about how they are really doing. And a common worry about who is struggling.

Picture a mother with a baby and a full-time job.

Up at 5:30. Day care drop-off. A full day of work. Bath, bottle, bed.

It's easy to assume she is the one most likely to be struggling.

To check, let's fill a room with every mother who answered the National Survey of Children's Health in 2023, 2024 or 2025. Hover over or tap any circle to meet her.

Every circle in this room is one real mother who answered the survey.

There are of them.

Each was asked: how would you rate your own mental or emotional health?

Most say excellent or very good. in 100 say fair or poor.

Read the circle like a pizza: each slice is one survey year, sized by how many mothers answered that year, and each ring is one answer, from excellent in the middle to poor at the crust.

Line them up by year and answer, and the pattern holds steady: in 2023, 2024 and 2025 alike, most mothers say excellent or very good, and a short column says poor.

Each year's columns differ in height because the survey reached a different number of mothers each year; the pattern within each year is what matters.

Now let them sort themselves by what they do all day.

In each group, ratings run from excellent in the center to poor at the rim, so the size of each colored ring shows how common that answer is.

The orange-red rim is thickest among mothers looking for work: in 100, almost twice the rate of mothers with full-time jobs ( in 100).

These mothers are poorer and less often married, which explains part of the gap, but not all of it.

And the fathers?

What the room can and can't tell us

The parents struggling most in this survey are the ones out of work, especially fathers, and money and family circumstances explain only part of it.

Full-time work, even with a baby at home, does not go with worse mental health for mothers here.

But a room is a snapshot. It can't show whether work shapes parents' mental health or mental health shapes work; most likely both. And this survey has no measure of postpartum depression or anxiety, no work hours, and no record of when mothers went back to work.

Methodology

Every circle is a real respondent. The room shows each mother (88,047) and father (43,550) who answered the National Survey of Children's Health in 2023, 2024 or 2025 as Adult 1, is the sampled child's biological or adoptive parent, and gave both an employment status and a mental health rating. Hovering shows that person's own answers: work, child's age, mental health rating and survey year. No names, IDs or locations are included; the data are from the public-use files.

Circles are counted as people; percentages are weighted. The survey samples some groups more than others (for example, families with young children in 2023), so counting circles does not exactly match the U.S. population. Every percentage in the text uses the survey weights, as in the main analysis. Because the weights describe children, "8 in 100 mothers" means the mothers of 8 in 100 children. Groups with fewer than 30 respondents are not reported.

Layout. Every circle sits the same distance from its neighbors. Within each group, parents are placed from "excellent" in the center to "poor" at the rim, so the area of each color is proportional to the number of respondents.

What this is not. These are unadjusted comparisons from a cross-sectional survey, not effects of employment. The chart version shows the adjusted models and confidence intervals.
